# Pitch Deck Structure — Research Brief

Sources are primary where reachable (Sequoia's own framework as widely reproduced, DocSend's own Startup Index PDF read directly), secondary/aggregator where the primary page 403'd or paywalled (marked accordingly).

## 1. Sequoia Capital — "Writing a Business Plan" deck (10 sections)

Sequoia's canonical outline, adapted from its "Writing a Business Plan" memo and reproduced across dozens of VC/accelerator guides:

1. **Company Purpose** — one declarative sentence: what do you do?
2. **Problem** — the customer pain, and how they cope with it today.
3. **Solution** — your value proposition; why it's 10x better, with use cases.
4. **Why Now** — the historical/technological/regulatory shift that makes this the moment.
5. **Market Size** — customer profile plus TAM/SAM/SOM.
6. **Competition** — the competitive set and your durable edge.
7. **Product** — line-up, core features, and roadmap.
8. **Business Model** — revenue model, pricing, distribution/sales motion.
9. **Team** — founders, key hires, advisors, and why they can win.
10. **Financials** — P&L, balance sheet, cash flow, cap table.

It has become the closest thing the industry has to a default schema — most later frameworks (YC's, DocSend's own template) are visible variations on this same spine. (Sequoia's original deck is not hosted at a stable Sequoia URL any more; reproduced by Alejandro Cremades, Waveup, MyPitchDecks, SlideGenius, and others.)

## 2. Y Combinator — seed deck outline + "one idea per slide"

YC's guidance (per its Startup Library "How to build your seed round pitch deck" and secondary summaries) converges on:
- **Length**: ~10–12 slides for a seed deck, built for fast reading, not a sales pitch you narrate line-by-line.
- **Three governing principles**: **Legibility, Simplicity, Obviousness.** Investors should never have to work to find the point of a slide.
- **"One idea per slide"**: a slide should carry the maximum one idea a person can process at a glance — cramming problem + solution + market on one slide defeats the format.
- **Content spine**: title/one-line description → problem → solution → business model → market size → team → the ask, with as little filler as the story allows. YC explicitly warns that most seed-stage companies don't have enough real substance to justify a longer deck, and stretching to fill slides muddies the story rather than strengthening it.

## 3. Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule — current vs. dated

- **10 slides** — force prioritization, cut fluff. Still holds up; it's basically the YC/DocSend length consensus (10–15 slides) arrived at a decade earlier.
- **20 minutes** — pitch in 20 minutes, leaving room for Q&A. Still sound advice for a live meeting; less relevant to a deck that's read cold and unsupervised (see §6).
- **30-point font** — force simple, uncluttered slides with minimal text per slide. The *intent* (no dense text walls, one point per slide) is exactly what DocSend's data later validated (best decks cap slide copy at ~50 words). The *literal* 30pt-font rule is the dated part: it predates the reading-deck/presenting-deck split, data-rich, chart-heavy slides, and the fact that ~sending a deck cold, unnarrated~ is now the default first touch with an investor (DocSend/PitchBook data) — a deck meant to be read alone at any font size needs more explanatory density than a projector slide read aloud ever did.

## 4. DocSend Startup Index — primary data (read directly from DocSend's own "The science behind developing a successful pitch deck" PDF)

**Deck-level averages:**
| Stage | Sections | Pages/slides | Avg. VC time on whole deck |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-seed | 12 | 18 | 4:10 |
| Seed | 12 | 19.5 | 3:20 |

(A separately-cited, more recent DocSend figure puts total average seed-deck viewing time at **1:56**, down 24% since 2021, with **21 seconds/slide** average and only **58% of decks viewed to completion** — attention has compressed further since the original Startup Index. Cold decks average 2:31 read time / 3–5% meeting conversion; **warm intros average 4:18 / 40–50% conversion** — the intro channel matters as much as the deck.)

**Per-section time (seed stage; the highest-attention sections, ranked):**
1. **Business Model — 64 sec** (highest of all; +28% YoY at pre-seed 2019→2020 as investors increasingly expect real monetization thinking at seed).
2. **Product — 59 sec** (3–5 slides; one of the most-scrutinized sections).
3. **Traction — 40 sec** (VCs spent **~80% *more* time** on traction in companies that ultimately did *not* get funded — extra scrutiny is a bad sign, not a good one).
4. **Team — 38 sec** (4th most-scrutinized at pre-seed, 5th at seed — important, but not the single highest-attention slide, contrary to some secondary write-ups).
5. Financials — 37 sec (only ~1/3 of successful seed decks even include it).
6. Problem / Solution / Competition — 34 sec each.
7. Market Size — 29 sec.
8. Fundraising Ask — 32 sec.
9. Company Purpose — 26 sec.
10. Why Now — 23 sec (lowest; also the least universally-included section — DocSend's advice is to cut it entirely if there's no genuine timing story).

**Slide order DocSend's data supports, and the "Why Now"/Market placement finding:** the walk-through order in successful decks is **Company Purpose → Problem → Solution → Why Now? → Product → Competition → Market Size → Team → Business Model → Traction → Financials → The Ask.** But DocSend adds an explicit, important caveat that founders should not read as literal page order: at the seed stage, **the Market Size section is "very closely linked to the purpose/problem/solution sections — these four should be thought of as tightly interwoven when hooking investors with your opening slides."** In other words, Why Now and Market Size are conceptually part of the *opening hook*, not a mid-deck data dump — even decks that place the Market Size *slide* mid-deck should make sure market timing and size logic is already implicit in how the Problem/Solution slides are framed. Why Now is optional: include only if there's a real, defensible timing catalyst (regulatory shift, technological unlock, macro event); otherwise cut it — it gets the least attention of any section and its presence isn't correlated with success.

**Other concrete DocSend tactics:** no table-of-contents slide (almost no successful deck has one); tag each slide with its section name so investors always know where they are; slide headlines are "valuable real estate" — make them a claim, not a label; cap slide copy at **50 words** (the team slide is usually the wordiest offender).

## 5. What the famous decks are each remembered for

- **Airbnb seed deck (2008, raised $600K from Sequoia)** — 14 slides, problem → solution → market validation → business model → competitive matrix → team → ask. Remembered for **radical simplicity plus real, if tiny, revenue traction** (it showed actual bookings from AirBed&Breakfast before asking for money) — proof of demand over projection, and for becoming the most-copied structural template in the industry.
- **Uber's original seed deck ("UberCab," ~2008)** — remembered for **positioning via analogy** ("NetJets for limos") aimed at a narrow initial wedge (black-car service for business travelers), paired with an aggressive best-case $1B+ revenue projection — a lesson in starting with a believable niche while still stating a huge ambition.
- **Front's Series A/B decks** — remembered for being **metric-native**: every strategic claim (funnel conversion, sales rep productivity, unit economics, capital efficiency) is translated into a specific number rather than a qualitative claim, which is exactly what DocSend's data says gets the most investor scrutiny (Business Model + Traction).
- **Buffer's seed deck (~$500K)** — remembered for **radical transparency**: real MRR, churn, CAC, and DAU numbers shown unhedged, trading polish for credibility.
- **Intercom's seed deck (~$600K)** — remembered for being **ultra-lean (8–10 slides) and product-led**: it spent its slides explaining *how the product works*, not just what it does, tailored tightly to a B2B SaaS/messaging narrative.

## 6. Deck-for-reading vs. deck-for-presenting

Two different documents optimized for two different jobs, because once a deck is on a screen or has been sent cold, investors read the slide instead of listening to the founder:
- **Reading/leave-behind deck** (sent cold, or left after a meeting): longer and denser — more words per slide, more explanatory context, and it's the version that carries an **appendix** (extra market data, cohort detail, security/compliance backup, extended financials) for follow-up diligence.
- **Presenting/live deck**: fewer words, larger visuals, no appendix in the live flow — the founder is the narration and the slide is a prompt, not the message.
- Rough length convention some advisors use: a **12–15 slide "teaser" deck** for cold intros, a **20–25 slide presentation deck** for live meetings, and a **30–50+ slide appendix** reserved for due diligence once a firm is genuinely interested. DocSend's own data (18–19.5 pages average) sits right in that 15–20 range and is a *read* deck, since most first investor contact today is a forwarded PDF, not a room.

## 7. What makes VCs stop reading

Repeated findings across investor-facing sources: investors assume they will stop at the **first red flag**, and the **first 3–4 slides decide whether the deck earns a second look** — most VCs decide within under three minutes whether it's worth continuing. Concrete stop-reading triggers:
- **Skipping the problem** — opening on a product tour instead of the pain; investors need the problem to feel expensive, frequent, and urgent before caring about the UI.
- **Walls of text** — violates "one idea per slide"; reads as unclear thinking, not thoroughness.
- **Top-down-only TAM** — a big market-research number with no bottom-up SAM/SOM logic (customer count × price × realistic penetration) is a classic credibility killer, and market-sizing claims generate the most investor follow-up questions of any section.
- **Weak or absent team slide, vague tagline, no clear go-to-market** — named directly among the mistakes that "kill deals."
- **Vague "use of funds"** — generic buckets ("marketing," "salaries") instead of funds tied to specific hires and milestones.
- **Traction that requires extra scrutiny to believe** — DocSend's own data shows VCs spent ~80% *more* time on the traction section of companies that did *not* get funded, i.e., traction that isn't immediately obvious invites skepticism rather than admiration.
- More subtly: decks that are visually polished and "structurally correct" but avoid all tension or unanswered questions can themselves read as evasive — investors notice what a deck conspicuously doesn't say about the founders' understanding of their own business.

## Distillation (~500 words)

Every major framework — Sequoia's 10-part "Writing a Business Plan" spine, Y Combinator's seed outline, and DocSend's data — converges on the same core sequence: **purpose → problem → solution → why now/market → product → business model → competition → team → traction → financials → ask.** Sequoia's version is the oldest and most reproduced; YC's contribution is less about ordering and more about discipline — ~10–12 slides, one idea per slide, governed by "legibility, simplicity, obviousness," because most seed-stage companies don't have enough real substance to justify padding. Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule (10 slides, 20 minutes, 30pt font) predates all of this data but got the spirit right — minimal text, one point per slide — even though the literal 30-point-font instruction is dated now that most decks are read cold and alone rather than narrated live.

DocSend's own Startup Index (read directly from their PDF) supplies the hard numbers: successful pre-seed decks run **18 pages / 12 sections / 4:10 avg. VC read time**; seed decks run **19.5 pages / 12 sections / 3:20**. More recent DocSend data shows total attention has since compressed to under two minutes with only 21 seconds/slide and 58% completion — and that **warm intros nearly double read time and 10x conversion** versus cold sends. Per-section attention is topped by **Business Model (64 sec)** and **Product (59 sec)**, not the market or team slide as popular belief holds; Team is 4th–5th most scrutinized, not first. Critically, DocSend explicitly says **Market Size and Why Now belong conceptually with the opening hook** — "tightly interwoven" with Purpose/Problem/Solution — even though they may sit mid-deck physically; Why Now is optional and should be cut if there's no real timing catalyst, since it gets the least attention of any section.

Famous decks each earn their reputation from one structural choice: Airbnb (real booking traction over projection), Uber (a narrow believable niche wrapped in a huge headline number), Front (every claim converted to a hard metric), Buffer (unhedged real numbers), Intercom (lean, product-led explanation over feature listing). Decks also split by job: a **dense, appendix-carrying reading deck** for cold sends and diligence vs. a **sparse, narrated presenting deck** for live meetings — conflating the two is itself a common failure. And investors report stopping at the first red flag, almost always within the first 3–4 slides: a buried problem statement, text-wall slides, top-down-only TAM with no bottom-up math, a vague "use of funds," or traction so unclear it invites suspicion rather than confidence.

### Recommended 15-slide order — pre-seed/seed B2B SaaS

1. **Cover** — what is this company, in one line?
2. **Company Purpose** — what do you ultimately do, and why does it matter?
3. **Problem** — what urgent, expensive pain does the target customer have today?
4. **Solution** — how do you solve that pain in a fundamentally better way?
5. **Why Now** — why is this the moment this becomes possible or inevitable?
6. **Product** — what does the product actually do, concretely?
7. **Traction** — what evidence shows this is already working?
8. **Market Size** — how big can this business become (TAM/SAM/SOM)?
9. **Business Model** — how, specifically, do you make money?
10. **Go-to-Market** — how will you acquire and scale customers repeatably?
11. **Competition** — why do you win against alternatives and the status quo?
12. **Team** — why is this team the one to solve it?
13. **Financials** — where do revenue, burn, and growth go over 2–3 years?
14. **The Ask** — how much are you raising, and what will it buy?
15. **Appendix / Contact** — what backup detail supports diligence, off the live read?
